# GTM Playbook by Stage

Contents: Stage 0–1 · B2B first customers · Stage 2 · Stage 3 · Network effects · Sources

## Stage 0–1: first 1,000 users (pre-PMF)

Goal: find the group that loves the product and pass the PMF gate. Users come from founder effort, not ads.

Principles (Paul Graham, *Do Things That Don't Scale*):
- Recruit users by hand. Stripe's "Collison installation": when someone agrees to try it, set them up on the spot.
- Measure weekly growth rate. 10%/week from 100 users is ~14,000 after one year.
- Delight each user personally; small startups can give service big companies cannot.
- Keep the fire contained: one narrow market first (Facebook = Harvard only).
- B2B: act as a consultant for one customer until the product fits them perfectly.
- Do manually what you will automate later.
- A big launch or a big-company partnership is not a growth plan.
- A founder does sales; hiring someone to do it comes later.

Seven tactics that account for ~99% of early consumer growth (Lenny Rachitsky, 100+ startups). Share = % of startups for which it mattered:

| Tactic | Share | Fits when | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Go where the ICP gathers (online/offline) | >50% | A community or venue already exists | Dropbox on Hacker News, Tinder on campus |
| Targeted strangers (email, DM, calls, door to door) | ~30% | Marketplace supply; each user is high value | DoorDash, Airbnb, Thumbtack |
| Friends and former colleagues | ~20% | Your network resembles the ICP | Yelp, LinkedIn |
| Press | >20% | A surprising story, a drumbeat of news | Airbnb, Udemy |
| Viral content | ~10% | Founder already has an audience | Duolingo TED talk, Calm |
| Physical placement | ~10% | Local products (food, dating) | DoorDash flyers |
| Influencers | — | Founder can reach the ICP's influencers | Instagram, Pinterest |

Archetypes: typical consumer → friends → gathering places → influencers. Marketplace → targeted strangers → physical placement → supply drives demand. Remarkable story → press → viral content. Pick 2–3, focus on the 1–2 that work, start from the circle closest to the founder.

Gate to Stage 2: ≥40% "very disappointed" in the core segment and flattening cohort retention. See `benchmarks.md`.

## B2B: first 10–100 customers

- List 100 accounts that match the ICP; founder does outreach, warm intros first.
- Sign 3–5 design partners: early use, weekly feedback, price concession in return.
- Charge early; paying customers give a cleaner PMF signal.
- Record every objection and the customer's exact words — raw material for the sales playbook.
- Other startups are the best early adopters; enterprise without connections means cold calls and introductions.

## Stage 2: 1K → 10K, find one repeatable channel

Bullseye framework (*Traction*, Weinberg and Mares). Spend 50% of time on product, 50% on traction.

19 channels: viral marketing, PR, unconventional PR, SEM, social and display ads, offline ads, SEO, content marketing, email marketing, engineering as marketing (free tools), targeting blogs, business development, sales, affiliate programs, existing platforms (app stores, marketplaces, other ecosystems), trade shows, offline events, speaking, community building.

Steps:
1. Brainstorm a concrete use of every channel: idea, 1–5 score, estimated CAC, reachable volume, time to implement.
2. Rank into inner circle, potential, long shots.
3. Cut the inner circle to 3.
4. Run cheap parallel tests — measuring potential, not chasing volume.
5. Focus on the one channel that moves the core number; repeat when it saturates.

Product–channel fit requirements (Brian Balfour):

| Channel | Product must have | Examples getting 70%+ of growth here |
|---|---|---|
| Virality | Short time-to-value; value prop broad across the user's network; better when the network joins | WhatsApp, Dropbox, Slack |
| Paid | Short time-to-value; value prop broad enough for ad targeting; transactional model that funds ads | Supercell, Squarespace |
| UGC SEO | Users create millions of unique public pages; motivation to contribute | TripAdvisor, Yelp, Pinterest |

Channel–model fit: ARPU sets the CAC ceiling. A few-dollar monthly plan cannot fund outbound sales.
Model–market fit: ARPU × customers in market × reachable share must match the ambition.

Also in Stage 2: tag every signup with its source, compare retention by channel cohort, optimize activation (define the aha moment), and for B2B turn founder-led sales into a written playbook before the first AE hire.

Gate to Stage 3: one channel brings most new users with room to grow; CAC payback stable over 3 consecutive cohorts; retention of that channel's users is not clearly worse than organic.

## Stage 3: 10K → 100K, build the growth engine

Only four long-term engines exist: paid, SEO, sales, virality. Consumer products effectively have three (sales rarely pays). Most startups grow mainly through one: Tinder via word of mouth, Calm via paid, Thumbtack via SEO. The founder's job: 2–3 kickstart tactics + become world-class at one engine.

| Engine | Signals it fits | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Virality (WOM, referral, invites, shared content) | Product improves as friends join; short time-to-value | Airbnb early: WOM >50% of guest and >70% of host growth |
| SEO | Users produce public content; proprietary data can generate thousands of pages; competitors win on SEO | Thumbtack: meaningful at month 18, primary channel by month 36 |
| Paid | LTV high enough to win auctions; fast conversion | Booking.com: AdWords from 2004, biggest growth source by 2008 |
| Sales | High B2B ACV, or high-value marketplace supply | Grubhub, OpenTable restaurant sales |

Marketplaces add "supply drives demand" (restaurants promoting DoorDash, Etsy sellers promoting shops). When the first engine plateaus, layer a second — usually paid, sometimes SEO.

Growth loops (Reforge): funnels run one way; loops reinvest each cycle's output as the next cycle's input. Ask "how does one cohort produce the next?" Fast-growing products run on 1–2 major loops. Example, Pinterest: signup → activate on relevant content → save/repin (quality signal) → content distributed to search → new user finds it → signup. Prefer an initiative giving 20 users growing 10%/week over one giving 500 users once. Organize a cross-functional team around the loop's output.

## Network effects (Andrew Chen, *The Cold Start Problem*)

Apply when the product is a marketplace, social, messaging, or collaboration tool.

1. **Cold start** — early on, network effects repel users. Build one atomic network that sustains itself (Zoom: 2 people; Slack: 3 users; Airbnb: hundreds of listings per market). Solve the hard side first.
2. **Tipping point** — replicate atomic networks with a repeatable playbook (per campus, city, team). Tactics: invite-only; come for the tool, stay for the network.
3. **Escape velocity** — strengthen acquisition (viral), engagement, and economic effects.
4. **Ceiling** — saturation, decaying channels, early-user churn, spam and fraud, lower-quality new users. New products usually drive the next curve.
5. **Moat** — challengers pick off niches; incumbents serve top users better and fast-follow.
